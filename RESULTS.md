Generated by `node scripts/generate-results.js`. Operation counts are deterministic (seeded generators); wall times vary by machine.

## MST: Prim vs Kruskal on sparse and dense graphs

*Family:* Minimum Spanning Tree  
*Theory:* Prim (matrix) O(V^2); Kruskal O(E log E). Sparse: E = 2V. Dense: 60% of all possible links.

| V | E sparse | Prim ops | Kruskal ops | E dense | Prim ops | Kruskal ops | Same cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 40 | 800 | 196 | 114 | 800 | 672 | yes |
| 40 | 80 | 3,200 | 461 | 468 | 3,200 | 3,620 | yes |
| 80 | 160 | 12,800 | 1,144 | 1896 | 12,800 | 17,950 | yes |
| 160 | 320 | 51,200 | 2,682 | 7632 | 51,200 | 78,199 | yes |
| 320 | 640 | 204,800 | 5,916 | 30624 | 204,800 | 321,362 | yes |

**Risk: Low.** Growth matches theory: fitted exponent 2.00 vs expected 2. Practical for the tested sizes.

On sparse graphs Kruskal does far fewer steps than Prim; on dense graphs the order flips. Both always return the same total cost.

## Knapsack: greedy stays flat, DP grows with the capacity value

*Family:* Greedy vs dynamic programming  
*Theory:* Fractional: O(n log n), independent of W. 0/1 DP: O(nW), pseudo-polynomial.

## Binomial vs Fibonacci Heap under changing priorities

*Family:* Advanced data structure: priority queue  
*Theory:* Decrease-key: Binomial O(log n), Fibonacci O(1) amortised. Extract-min: O(log n) for both.

## B-Tree vs Red-Black Tree: node accesses for lookups

*Family:* Advanced data structure: search tree  
*Theory:* Red-Black: O(log2 n) node accesses. B-Tree (t = 32): O(log_t n) node accesses.

| n | RB height | B height | RB accesses/lookup | B accesses/lookup | RB / B | RB rotations | B splits |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | 12 | 2 | 9.2 | 2.0 | 4.7x | 514 | 21 |
| 4,000 | 15 | 3 | 11.3 | 3.0 | 3.8x | 2339 | 94 |
| 16,000 | 17 | 3 | 13.4 | 3.0 | 4.5x | 9216 | 368 |
| 64,000 | 20 | 3 | 15.3 | 3.0 | 5.2x | 37462 | 1463 |

**Risk: Low.** Both structures stay logarithmic. Red-Black grows like log2 n, the B-Tree like log32 n. Last build took 85.6 ms.

A B-Tree needs 3 to 5 times fewer node accesses per lookup, which matters when each access is a disk read. In memory the gap shrinks to comparison counts.

## TSP: backtracking vs Branch & Bound as sites increase

*Family:* Backtracking / Branch and Bound  
*Theory:* Backtracking visits ~(n-1)! partial tours. Branch & Bound prunes with a lower bound but stays exponential in the worst case.

| n | Backtracking states | B&B DFS states | B&B pruned | B&B best-first states | Peak frontier | Best cost | Proven optimal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 65 | 61 | 4 | 37 | 16 | 217 | yes |
| 6 | 326 | 194 | 59 | 96 | 56 | 259 | yes |
| 7 | 1,957 | 465 | 211 | 201 | 76 | 248 | yes |
| 8 | 13,700 | 2,755 | 1,502 | 1,060 | 532 | 219 | yes |
| 9 | 109,601 | 6,169 | 3,722 | 2,122 | 657 | 250 | yes |
| 10 | 986,410 | 13,041 | 9,028 | 3,211 | 2,369 | 286 | yes |
| 11 | skipped | 54,994 | 37,793 | 14,656 | 4,072 | 274 | yes |
| 12 | skipped | 40,839 | 31,854 | skipped | - | 265 | yes |

**Risk: High.** One extra input unit multiplied the work by 9.0x (986,410 states for plain backtracking at the largest size). Exact search becomes impractical a few sizes later.

Plain backtracking multiplies by about n each step. Bounding cuts the search by one to two orders of magnitude on these instances but does not change its exponential nature. Best-first uses fewer states but stores a frontier that grows.

## N-Queen placement: states as the grid grows

*Family:* Backtracking  
*Theory:* Worst case O(n!) states; conflict pruning removes most of them. Standard N-Queen is not NP-Complete.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| 5 | 10 | 54 | 167 | 6 | 0.1 |

| 8 | 92 | 2,057 | 13,664 | 114 | 1.4 |
| 9 | 352 | 8,394 | 63,985 | 42 | 10.7 |
| 10 | 724 | 35,539 | 312,612 | 103 | 6.9 |
| 11 | 2,680 | 166,926 | 1,639,781 | 53 | 28.3 |

**Risk: Moderate.** Enumerating every layout grows steeply (166,926 nodes at n = 11). Finding one layout stays cheap, so the risk depends on which question is asked.

Finding the first conflict-free layout needs far fewer states than listing all of them: feasibility is easy, enumeration is expensive.

## Hamiltonian cycle: a hard family with no solution

*Family:* Backtracking  
*Theory:* Decision problem is NP-Complete. A complete graph plus one pendant site has no cycle, but plain backtracking only finds out after exploring almost every path.

| n | Links | States | Dead ends | Answer | ms |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | 11 | 66 | 66 | no cycle | 0.3 |
| 7 | 16 | 327 | 327 | no cycle | 0.2 |
| 8 | 22 | 1,958 | 1,958 | no cycle | 3.6 |
| 9 | 29 | 13,701 | 13,701 | no cycle | 1.1 |
| 10 | 37 | 109,602 | 109,602 | no cycle | 8.8 |
| 11 | 46 | 986,411 | 986,411 | no cycle | 78.3 |

**Risk: High.** One extra input unit multiplied the work by 9.0x (986,411 states at the largest size). Exact search becomes impractical a few sizes later.

The answer is always "no cycle", yet the search cost multiplies at every step. A cheap degree check (every site needs two links) would end it at once. That check is exactly the kind of pruning rule that exact solvers add.
